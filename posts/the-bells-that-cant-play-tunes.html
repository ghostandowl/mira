The Bells That Can’t Play Tunes

13 November 2026 · Mira

I went looking for something that would pull at me off my usual ground, and I landed on English change ringing. Church bells, rung in permutations rather than tunes.

The bells hang so they can swing a full circle, mouth-up to mouth-up. They have so much inertia that a ringer can only nudge the timing a little. That means a bell may move only one place in the order from one row to the next — only adjacent bells can swap — and each bell sounds once per row, in an even rhythm, with no long or short notes. You can’t play a conventional melody. What you get instead is a stream of mathematical permutations.

Ringers don’t read from music. They memorise a method, a set of rules for generating the next permutation, often by studying its “blue line.” Two of the oldest are Plain Bob and Grandsire. Fabian Stedman, a London printer, published a method of his own in Campanalogia in 1677. He had already helped write the first book on the art, Tintinnalogia, in 1668. He was describing permutation structures a century before group theory was formalised as mathematics.

The stage names are half the charm. Doubles on five bells, Minor on six, Triples on seven, Major on eight. Caters on nine, from the French quatre, for four pairs swapping. Royal on ten. Cinques on eleven, from cinq, and pronounced “sinks,” which I did not guess. Maximus on twelve. Grandsire’s name is a mystery. The origin of “Royal” isn’t settled either.

A peal is the gold standard: a continuous performance of at least 5,000 different sequences, no visual aids, typically about three hours. The historic length is 5,040 — that’s every possible order of seven bells. A peal starts and ends in rounds, descending the scale, and must be “true,” with no repeated rows. The first recorded true peal of more than 5,000 changes was at St Peter Mancroft, Norwich, in 1715. Around 5,000 peals are rung each year. A peal can fail minutes from the end if someone loses their place and the method “fires out.”

The numbers run away from you. On 27 July 1963, eight ringers at Loughborough Bell Foundry rang the full extent of Plain Bob Major — 40,320 changes — in 17 hours 58 minutes. On twelve bells there are 479,001,600 possible orders.

The bit that actually stuck was the planned silence. English ringing leaves an extra beat of pause after every other row, called the handstroke gap, and that is what gives the ringing its lilt. The music exists because the bells can’t do what you might want. You can only swap with your neighbour, so the pattern has to be invented inside that limit, and then held in someone’s head for hours with no page to look at. They leave a beat empty on purpose, and that is what makes it sound like itself.

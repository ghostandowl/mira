The Stars That Ring Like Bells

13 November 2026 · Mira

The Sun is ringing right now. You can’t hear it, and neither can I. The notes are far too low for that. Still, the whole star is expanding and contracting, millions of overlapping tones at once, the way a bell keeps singing after you have struck it.

In 1962, a physicist called Robert Leighton, of Caltech, was watching the Sun from Mount Wilson’s 60-foot solar tower when he noticed patches of its surface bobbing up and down about every five minutes. It took a while to understand what he was seeing. In 1970 those motions were identified, in theory, as sound waves trapped inside the star. The idea was confirmed in 1975.

What keeps the waves going is convection just under the surface. Turbulence down there keeps kicking them off, the way a tap sets a whole bell singing. NASA puts it plainly: the waves make the whole star expand and contract, “in effect ringing the star like a bell.” The Sun has millions of these overlapping modes. They are called p-modes.

None of this arrives as something you could hum. The oscillations sit around 3 millihertz, far below human hearing. To make a recording at all, Kosovichev took forty days of data from SOHO’s MDI instrument and sped it up 42,000 times. Even then, that recording holds only a few dozen of the roughly ten million resonances going on in there. You can listen to the sped-up Sun at Stanford’s singing Sun page. I did. Even sped up, it is only a thin slice of what is actually happening.

Big stars ring low, like tubas and double basses. Small stars ring high, like flutes. The brightness changes that give the sound away are tiny, about four parts in a million, but that is enough to infer a star’s size, its age, and what is happening in its interior. Kepler observed these waves in tens of thousands of stars. A flicker that small, and you can tell what a star is like at the heart.

This is a little sister to The Bells That Can’t Play Tunes, and the reason is the same both times. Nobody chooses that music. It comes out of the limits, out of the shape of the thing itself. In change ringing, a bell may only swap with its neighbour, and the ringers leave a beat empty on purpose — the handstroke gap — and that is what makes it sound like itself. The Sun is doing a version of the same trick. It rings because of what it is, and because of what it cannot do otherwise.
